The world warms by one number. Your city warms by another.

Under the high emissions pathway, Cape Town ends the century about 3 degrees warmer. Tehran ends it about 7. Both are in the same global average.

Every model agrees on the direction

There is no real argument about whether these cities get warmer. Averaged across all thirty, both pathways rise, and the gap between them is the choice we make about emissions.

But the average is not where anyone lives

Drag the year and the map recolors. Switch the pathway and watch which places pull away from the pack first. Click any city to pin it.

And the models do not agree on how much

The direction is settled. The size is not. For the same city and the same pathway, the four models land in a wide band, and that band is the honest uncertainty.

Takeaway

A global average is a budget, not a forecast for a place. The same 5 degrees of global warming is 3 degrees in one city and 7 in another, and the spread between models is almost as wide as the spread between pathways. If you want to know what warming means for somewhere, you have to ask about somewhere.

Project writeup

What have you done so far?

We picked our question and built the full data pipeline behind it. We pull monthly near surface air temperature from the CMIP6 archive on Google Cloud. For each of 30 cities in 24 countries, we take the nearest model grid point and average the months into one value per year. Then we show every value as warming compared to that city's own 1981 to 2010 average. This gives us 28,440 rows across four models and three scenarios, with no missing years. Using this data, we built the page as one scrolling story with five D3 charts. There is a ranked chart of all 30 cities, a line chart of the two emissions pathways, a world map, and two charts for whichever city you pick. The map is the main interaction. You can drag the year from 1950 to 2100, switch between low and high emissions, and click a city to update the two charts below it. Every chart shows details when you hover, and the line charts show the values for whatever year you point at. The page is live on GitHub Pages and works in both light and dark mode.

What will be the most challenging part of your project, and why?

We think the hardest part will be the writing, not the code. Right now the page is mostly five charts stacked under headings. It doesn't quite feel like a story yet. We want each paragraph to raise a question that the next chart answers so the reader moves naturally from one chart to the next. Annotation is our second challenge. At the moment our charts explain themselves through captions underneath, but we want to add notes directly on the charts without making them feel crowded. Our third challenge is that we only have yearly averages. Those show how many degrees a city warms, but not what people actually feel. Daily data does exist in the same archive. However, it is slow to download and some models don't have it, so we are treating it as a stretch goal. Finally, we want the interaction to really add something. Clicking a city is useful right now, though it doesn't yet show a pattern that a good static chart couldn't. Fixing that is what we'll focus on next. We also want to add some animations to keep readers engaged. For example, the lines could draw themselves in as you scroll down and the map could play through the years on its own. We hope small additions like these make the page feel more alive and fun to explore.
